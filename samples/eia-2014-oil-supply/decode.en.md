# Global liquid fuels supply outlook and risks

**Classification**: Industry, Commodities

**Completion status**: Complete

U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), February 2014. The report credits Asmeret Asghedom and Samuel Gorgen as principal contributors. [Original report](source.pdf)

## Overview

EIA expected global petroleum and other liquid fuels supply to grow in 2014 and 2015, led by producers outside the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC). The report starts from a forecast miss in 2013 and examines what could move supply away from the new baseline. It looks at unplanned outages, how much spare capacity can offset them, drilling productivity, project delays, and maintenance. Upside and downside refer to supply relative to the report's forecast.

## The supply baseline and the 2013 forecast miss

The table shows EIA's forecasts as of February 2014. The figures are year-over-year changes in supply, in million barrels per day. EIA expected record growth from non-OPEC producers in 2014, with the United States contributing more than half of that growth. It expected Saudi Arabia and some other OPEC producers to cut output as supply outside the group rose. [PDF page 3, summary](source.pdf#page=3)

| Supply group | 2014 forecast | 2015 forecast |
|---|---:|---:|
| Global petroleum and other liquid fuels | Increase of 1.7 | Increase of 1.4 |
| Non-OPEC producers | Increase of 1.9 | Increase of 1.5 |
| OPEC producers | Decrease of almost 0.2 | Decrease of more than 0.1 |

**Historical comparison.** The January 2013 outlook projected global supply growth of **1.0 million barrels per day** for 2013. The February 2014 report estimated the same year's growth at **about 0.6 million barrels per day**. EIA attributed most of that gap to more unplanned outages among OPEC producers than it had expected. Planned maintenance can also widen a forecast miss if complications push back the restart date. ## OPEC outages and spare capacity

EIA puts OPEC's 2013 unplanned crude oil outages at an average of nearly 1.8 million barrels per day, almost twice the previous year's level. Outages reached 2.6 million barrels per day at year-end as disruptions in Libya increased. Figure 1 uses estimated shut-in volumes relative to effective production capacity. The table gives the country averages cited in the text. [PDF page 3, Figure 1](source.pdf#page=3)

| Producer | 2013 average unplanned outage, million barrels per day |
|---|---:|
| Libya | 0.7 |
| Iran | 0.6 |
| Nigeria | 0.3 |
| Iraq | 0.2 |

**Key assumption.** EIA treats these four producers as having no spare crude oil production capacity. When disruptions grow, members with spare capacity, led by Saudi Arabia, typically raise production in EIA's assessment. That cushions the immediate supply loss and draws down the spare capacity available for further disruptions. [PDF page 4](source.pdf#page=4)

### Libya has room to recover from an already low baseline

Strikes at export terminals and protests around fields and pipelines restricted production in the second half of 2013. Libyan crude output averaged 1.4 million barrels per day from January to May and fell to 0.2 million in the final two months. Libya accounted for half of OPEC's fourth-quarter outages. Restarting the 340,000-barrel-per-day El Sharara field lifted national output to an average of 0.5 million barrels per day in January 2014. A pipeline blockage reduced it again in February. Fields serving the Es Sider, Ras Lanuf, and Zueitina terminals had largely been shut since mid-2013.

EIA links a recovery to negotiations with groups blocking production in the west and progress with the movement seeking autonomy in the central-eastern region. The planned drafting of a constitution adds another source of uncertainty. The baseline keeps output constrained through 2014 and assumes partial recovery in 2015. With production and exports already depressed, EIA sees Libya mainly as an upside risk to global supply. [PDF page 4](source.pdf#page=4)

### Theft and pipeline interruptions weigh on Nigeria's outlook

Nigeria produced less than 2.0 million barrels per day on average in 2013, its lowest output since 2009. Unplanned outages exceeded 0.3 million barrels per day on average. Shell repeatedly shut the Nembe Creek Trunkline and the Trans Niger Pipeline to repair leaks attributed to theft. The closures deferred production destined for the Bonny terminal. Unions withdrew a recent shutdown threat after the government ruled out privatizing existing refineries. Renewed threats from the Movement for the Emancipation of the Niger Delta (MEND) and uncertainty ahead of the 2015 presidential election remained sources of disruption.

The baseline keeps output below effective capacity of 2.2 to 2.3 million barrels per day. A few small projects were due within two years, and major starts were scheduled after 2015. EIA concludes that capacity is likely to be flat at best during the forecast period and could revise production down if theft and sabotage persist. [PDF pages 4–5](source.pdf#page=4)

### Core oil sanctions shape Iran's investment and exports

EIA estimates that Iranian crude production averaged 2.8 million barrels per day in 2013, down from nearly 3.7 million in 2011. Sanctions constrained sales and investment needed to offset declines at existing wells. The Joint Plan of Action was announced on November 24, 2013 and implemented starting January 20, 2014. It suspended restrictions on associated insurance and transport services while core oil sanctions remained in force. Countries still importing Iranian oil already had arrangements to deal with the insurance restrictions.

EIA keeps its baseline assumption of steadily falling production into 2015. An increase in sales and output would depend on negotiations with the P5+1, the five permanent UN Security Council members plus Germany, and on the oil restrictions retained by the United States and European Union. EIA planned to monitor monthly crude exports as a possible reason to revise the forecast. [PDF page 5](source.pdf#page=5)

### Iraq's export bottlenecks make both outcomes possible

EIA estimates crude production at 2.60 million barrels per day in 2011, 2.96 million in 2012, and 3.03 million in 2013. Pipeline attacks in the north and planned work at southern terminals in the last four months of 2013 held output back. Unplanned outages in 2013 averaged about 0.2 million barrels per day, largely from repeated disruptions on the Kirkuk–Ceyhan pipeline. Southern exports were near capacity, and refining also faced bottlenecks. Aging pipelines limited northern exports, and the deteriorating Strategic Pipeline restricted how much oil could be transferred north from southern Iraq.

The baseline extends a pace of growth similar to 2011–2013 over the next two years and remains below the Iraqi government's target of more than 4.0 million barrels per day. Restoring existing infrastructure and expanding southern export capacity could lift production above the baseline. Slow expansion or more outages as political disputes intensify could pull it below. [PDF page 5](source.pdf#page=5)

## Non-OPEC disruptions and effective capacity

Non-OPEC liquids production grew by an estimated nearly 1.4 million barrels per day in 2013. Unplanned disruptions averaged almost 0.8 million barrels per day in 2013, down from more than 0.9 million in 2012 and above the 2011 level of less than 0.5 million. Sudan and South Sudan, Syria, and Yemen accounted for 70% of non-OPEC disruption volumes in 2013. Brazil, Canada, China, and Colombia accounted for most of the rest. Figure 2 shows the estimates in thousands of barrels per day and uses the same effective-capacity basis as Figure 1. [PDF page 6, Figure 2](source.pdf#page=6)

- **Syria and Yemen.** In the fourth quarter of 2013, Syria had nearly 290,000 barrels per day offline and Yemen had 100,000. EIA expected both countries to remain major contributors to disruptions over the next two years. It also expected effective production capacity to keep falling because of damage to reservoirs, fields, and infrastructure.
- **Sudan and South Sudan.** South Sudan's estimated disruption fell from about 330,000 barrels per day in the first quarter of 2013 to less than 100,000 in the fourth quarter. Both higher production and a downward revision to effective capacity explain the decline. In December, the conflict intensified and some fields shut in or reduced output. EIA projected combined liquids production of 300,000–350,000 barrels per day during the outlook, below the pre-shut-in level of 460,000 in 2011. The escalation in South Sudan made its supply risk mainly downside.

## U.S. onshore and Gulf of Mexico production

### Faster drilling improvements could lift U.S. onshore output above forecast

Earlier U.S. production forecasts had undershot output because onshore drilling productivity improved faster than EIA anticipated. The baseline puts onshore oil production in the contiguous 48 states at 5.7 million barrels per day in 2013 and 7.1 million in 2015. Continued improvements in drilling and hydraulic fracturing of tight formations could push productivity higher than assumed. EIA sees the main onshore uncertainty as upside to supply. [PDF page 7](source.pdf#page=7)

### Gulf production depends on project schedules, weather, and maintenance

EIA projects average oil output in the federal Gulf of Mexico rising from 1.3 million barrels per day in 2013 to 1.6 million in 2015. The estimate uses existing and planned projects, with start dates and peak volumes based on what operators disclose. Delays move expected volumes into later periods. The productivity of new wells after they begin operating adds another uncertainty. [PDF page 7](source.pdf#page=7)

**Weather assumption.** EIA uses hurricane forecasts from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). Above-average activity had been forecast for 2013, but the only significant hurricane-related outage EIA records that year was Hurricane Karen in October. It took an average of 100,000 barrels per day offline that month, approximately 3 million barrels in total. The 2014 and 2015 forecasts assume average hurricane outages. Actual volumes shut in could be higher or lower. [PDF pages 7–8, Figure 3](source.pdf#page=8)

**Maintenance risk.** Figure 3 separates forecast hurricane outages, actual hurricane outages, and actual maintenance outages, in thousands of barrels per day. Several 2013 disruptions came from infrastructure work, including maintenance at the Pascagoula gas processing plant in May and June, platform installation for Atlantis Phase 2 in June, and routine maintenance in August. Limited public notice of this work makes it a recurring downside risk to offshore output. [PDF pages 7–8, Figure 3](source.pdf#page=8)

## Kashagan and North Sea maintenance

### Kazakhstan's supply growth depends on the Kashagan restart

EIA describes Kashagan as the largest oil field discovered in the preceding 35 years. It had faced repeated delays and two failed starts in the second half of 2013. EIA assumes a restart in the second half of 2014 with higher output in 2015. Technical challenges and development costs are expected to keep production below the first-phase target of 370,000 barrels per day. That schedule underlies Kazakhstan's forecast average annual increase of 110,000 barrels per day in liquid fuels supply over the next two years. A delay in commercial production beyond 2015 would lower the forecast for Kazakhstan's growth. [PDF page 8](source.pdf#page=8)

### The scope and duration of North Sea maintenance are hard to track

Maintenance on fields, pipelines, gas processing facilities, and terminals usually takes place from May through September. More than 200,000 barrels per day of liquid fuels supply is typically offline on average during that season. Work affects supplies of Brent, Forties, Oseberg, and Ekofisk (BFOE), the crude grades behind the Brent benchmark. Work at gas processing facilities also affects associated fields.

The report cites the International Energy Agency (IEA) on the importance of the length and scope of maintenance. Operators face no disclosure requirement for these plans, which makes both difficult to track. Mature fields and technical problems can also delay restarts or require additional work. These factors make the amount and duration of lost supply uncertain. [PDF page 8](source.pdf#page=8)
